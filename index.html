On an island, garage doors fail differently

Coronado sits in salt air year round, and the hardware on a garage door is mostly bare steel. Torsion springs, cables, hinges, and roller bearings corrode faster here than they do eight miles inland. A spring rated for ten thousand cycles can give out well short of that once rust gets into the coils.

If your door is a few years old and starting to sound wrong, corrosion is usually the first thing worth ruling out.

Worth a look before it breaks

  • Rust blooming on the springs above the door, or flaking off the coils
  • Frayed or kinked lift cables running up either side
  • Grinding or squealing from the rollers as the door travels
  • The door lifting unevenly, one side higher than the other
  • An opener that strains, stops short, or reverses on its own
  • Bottom-panel rot or rust on a wood or steel door facing the water

What we handle

Most requests come down to one of six things. If yours isn't listed, describe it in the form.

Broken springs

The most common failure, and the one you shouldn't attempt yourself. A torsion spring holds enough stored energy to break bones.

Opener repair and replacement

Dead motors, stripped gears, bad sensors, and remotes that stopped pairing. Sometimes it's the opener, sometimes it's the door fighting it.

Cables and rollers

Snapped cables, doors hanging crooked, rollers seized in the track. Frequently corrosion-driven on the island.

Off-track doors

Usually follows a cable failure or a bumper tap. The door needs to come down safely before anything gets re-seated.

Panel and section replacement

One dented or rusted-through panel doesn't always mean a whole new door, depending on the make and how old it is.

New door installation

When repair stops making sense — or when the bottom section has finally rusted out and you want something built for coastal exposure.
