Spring repair and replacement

Torsion springs sit on a bar above the door and do almost all the lifting. The opener only guides the door; the springs carry the weight. When one breaks you'll usually hear a bang like a gunshot, and afterwards the door either won't lift at all or feels impossibly heavy.

Springs are rated in cycles — one up-and-down is a cycle. Standard springs run about 10,000. In Coronado, corrosion often ends them earlier. If your door has two springs and one has gone, the other is the same age and typically gets replaced at the same time.

What matters here

  • Do not try to replace a torsion spring yourself. The stored energy is enough to cause serious injury.
  • Don't run the opener on a broken spring — you'll burn out the motor or bend the door.
  • If you need the car out, there's a safe manual release, but the door will be very heavy. Ask about it when you request a quote before trying.

Opener repair and replacement

Openers fail in a few recognizable ways: the motor hums but nothing moves (stripped drive gear), the door reverses right after touching the floor (limit or force settings), it goes down a foot and comes back up (blocked or misaligned safety sensors), or the remotes simply stop working.

Sensor problems are worth checking yourself first — the two small units near the floor on either side of the door need to see each other, and a bike or a stack of boxes can break the beam. If the indicator light on one is off or flickering, they're misaligned.

Repair or replace

  • Under about ten years old with one failed part — usually worth repairing.
  • Older chain-drive units with multiple issues — replacement is often cheaper over time.
  • Belt-drive openers run noticeably quieter, which matters in Coronado's attached garages and shared walls.
  • Any opener made before 1993 lacks modern safety reversal. Replace it.

Cables and rollers

Lift cables run from the bottom bracket of the door up to drums on the spring bar. They're under tension whenever the door is down. Salt air gets into the strands, rust works outward, and eventually a cable frays through — often leaving the door hanging crooked or jammed.

Rollers are the wheels that run in the track. Steel rollers with exposed bearings seize up on the island faster than most owners expect. Nylon rollers with sealed bearings hold up better here and run quieter.

Signs to act on

  • Visible fraying, kinks, or rust streaks on either cable
  • The door sitting noticeably lower on one side
  • Grinding or shrieking as the door travels
  • A cable that has come off its drum entirely

Off-track doors

A door comes off its track after a cable snaps, a roller fails, or something hits it — a bumper, a ladder, a gust catching an open door. The panels end up jammed at an angle with the weight distributed in ways the hardware was never meant to hold.

This one genuinely needs hands off. The door has to be secured and lowered under control before anything is re-seated, and forcing it usually bends panels or pulls the track out of the wall, turning a moderate repair into a door replacement.

Until someone arrives

  • Don't operate the opener. Not even to "see if it still works."
  • Keep children and pets out of the garage.
  • If a car is trapped inside, say so in the form — it changes the priority.

Panel and section replacement

Doors are built in horizontal sections, and damage is usually confined to one. A dented bottom panel from a bumper, or a rusted-through bottom section on a door that faces the water, doesn't automatically mean a whole new door.

Whether a single section can be swapped depends on the manufacturer, the model, and how old the door is — discontinued lines are the usual sticking point. On a door older than about fifteen years, matching sections often can't be sourced, and replacement becomes the practical route.

Bottom panels on the island

  • Bottom sections take the worst of the salt spray and standing water
  • Steel doors rust from the inside of the panel outward, so it looks fine until it doesn't
  • Wood doors facing the bay swell, split, and lose their bottom seal
  • A failed bottom seal lets water in and accelerates everything above it

New door installation

When the cost of repairs starts approaching the cost of a door, or when the bottom sections have rusted out, replacement makes sense. The decision usually comes down to age, how much of the hardware is corroded, and whether sections can still be sourced for the model.

Coronado's exposure is worth factoring into the choice. Doors and hardware specified for coastal use — galvanized or powder-coated hardware, sealed-bearing rollers, corrosion-resistant springs, composite or fiberglass panels instead of raw steel — cost more up front and meaningfully outlast the standard spec here.

Worth asking your installer

  • Is the hardware galvanized or coated for coastal exposure?
  • Are the springs corrosion-resistant, and what cycle rating?
  • Sealed-bearing nylon rollers rather than open steel?
  • What's the warranty, and does it exclude corrosion?
  • Does the quote include hauling away the old door?
